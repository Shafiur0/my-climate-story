The problem

Climate change is taught as a planet.
People live in a place.

Global averages are true, and almost useless in a classroom in Satkhira. A student there does not feel 1.28 °C. They feel a hotter September, a river that moved two hundred metres, a monsoon that arrives late.

Most climate tools are English-first, data-heavy, and assume fast internet and a technical background. The people who will live with the consequences longest have the fewest tools to understand what is happening where they stand.

So we built the opposite: a local, plain-language, low-bandwidth app where every claim is traceable to a NASA dataset — and where the app is allowed to say “we don't know yet.”

Why this is different

Most climate apps show you a number. This one shows you the receipt.

Hyperlocal, not global

Your coordinates, your grid cell, your September. A global average has never told anyone what happened in their own field.

Evidence you can audit

Every number opens an Evidence Passport: source, period, sample size, method, uncertainty and caveat. Nothing is asserted without a trace.

Honest about uncertainty

When the data cannot support a claim, the app says “no reliable conclusion yet.” That is a feature, not a failure.

Built for a weak signal

Static data cards, compressed imagery and a lite mode. No 3D globe to download. It opens on a slow phone in a classroom.

Trend lab

Twelve months. Four places. Which one is actually changing?

Annual averages hide the story. This grid runs a separate Theil–Sen trend for every calendar month at every pilot site, then corrects all 48 p-values together with Benjamini–Hochberg. Hover any cell.

Cooler
Warmer q < 0.05

Hover a cell to inspect the test.

Why September

In the 2001–2024 window, September is the only month that warms significantly at all four sites. That is a lead worth chasing, not a conclusion.

Why correct 48 tests

Run enough tests and something will look significant by luck. The q-value asks: after accounting for all 48 attempts, is this still surprising?

Why the 40-year view disagrees

The 1985–2024 window shows apparent cooling for several months. That is a reanalysis artefact signal, not evidence of a colder Bangladesh. We show it rather than hide it.

Method & limitations

How we know, and where we stop knowing.

A climate claim is only as good as its paperwork. This page is the paperwork.

01

The question

Has September near-surface air temperature changed across different geographic settings in Bangladesh from 2001 to 2024 — and does the pattern survive a check for data quality and multiple testing?

02

Retrieve

NASA POWER monthly T2M and PRECTOTCORR, 1985–2024, at four pilot coordinates. 1,920 monthly records, raw units preserved.

03

Estimate

Theil–Sen slope in °C per decade, with a 95% bootstrap interval. Robust to outliers in a way an ordinary least-squares fit is not.

04

Test

Kendall rank correlation for monotonic trend, then Benjamini–Hochberg across all 48 site–month combinations per period.

05

Challenge it

Repeat with a second start year. Inspect the 1985–2024 window for discontinuities. Compare against an independent record before publishing.

Data-quality finding

The 40-year comparison does not say what it looks like it says.

Comparing 1985–1994 with 2015–2024 in the raw NASA POWER series implies cooling at all four sites, alongside implausibly large rainfall increases. For Dhaka, the ten-year mean shifts from about 26.49 °C to 25.51 °C, while modelled annual rainfall moves from roughly 1,101 mm to 2,809 mm.

Those numbers are arithmetically correct outputs from the downloaded data. They are not an established forty-year climate trend. Reanalysis depends on changing input observations, model versions and correction procedures, and the series shows step changes consistent with product transitions. Until that is resolved against independent observations, the app reports no reliable conclusion yet for this window.

What this app does not claim

  • That any observed river movement was caused by global climate change.
  • That POWER values are thermometer readings at your house.
  • That a two-date image pair is a time series.
  • That Bangladesh results generalise to every delta on Earth.

Known limitations

  • POWER is modelled reanalysis on a coarse grid, not a station measurement.
  • MODIS true colour begins in 2000; a true 1980s pair needs Landsat.
  • Monsoon cloud cover removes usable scenes from many dates.
  • Serial autocorrelation has not yet been fully corrected for.

AI use disclosure

How we used AI — named, explained, and limited.

Space Apps requires every AI tool in a solution to be declared, with its reasoning and the data it worked on. Here is ours, in full.

Tool
WorkBuddy AI, an agentic coding assistant, used for front-end implementation and copy drafting. No other generative AI is embedded in the product.
What it worked on
Interface code, the SVG chart engine, layout and written copy. It did not generate any climate value, statistic or trend shown in the app.
Reasoning
A 48-hour build rewards time spent on data validity over time spent on markup. AI compressed the implementation so the team could spend its hours on the statistics and the honesty checks.
Where the numbers come from
Every figure is a calculation on NASA POWER and GISTEMP values, reproducible from the public repository. The Evidence Passport on each story shows the inputs.
Prompts and verification
The working prompt log is kept with the project documentation. All generated output was reviewed line by line before commit, and no claim was accepted without a traceable calculation behind it.
